# Financial Education Curriculum — Sources, Paths, and Quiz Bank

Read this file when teaching. It lists only primary or expert-maintained
sources that are free to read, with what each is good for, and it orders
them into learning paths. Nothing here is investment advice.

## How to use this curriculum

1. Place the learner: ask two or three questions from the Quiz bank at the
   level they name (or Level 1 if unsure). Do not lecture before placing.
2. Teach one concept per turn: definition, one worked number, one source
   link, one check question. Wait for the answer before moving on.
3. Prefer the learner's own numbers (with their consent, via the
   portfolio-snapshot or dividend-income skills) over invented examples.
4. Every factual claim gets a source from this file; if the point needs a
   source not listed here, say so and name the source explicitly.
5. End each session with a two-line recap and the next item on the path.

## Source quality standard

Sources qualify when they are (a) a regulator, government body, or
central bank; (b) a university course or a professor's public materials;
(c) a professional body's education program; (d) a peer-reviewed paper
or a widely cited working paper; or (e) a community wiki with editorial
review and citations (Bogleheads). Commercial sites are listed only as
secondary references and must be checked against a primary source.

Red flags that disqualify a source: promises of returns, a product to sell
inside the lesson, no author or date, no citations, affiliate links for
brokers or courses, or performance claims without a benchmark.

## Level 1: Foundations

Goal: the learner can explain compound interest, the difference between
saving and investing, what an index fund is, and what fees cost.

1. Compound interest and the time value of money. Read: Investor.gov
   compound interest calculator (https://www.investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator);
   Khan Academy "Interest and debt". Worked number: 10,000 at 7% for 30
   years is 76,123; at 6% (one point of fees) it is 57,435.
2. Emergency fund, debt, and order of operations. Read: Bogleheads
   "Prioritizing investments" (https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Prioritizing_investments);
   CFPB debt tools.
3. Account types: taxable, 401(k), traditional and Roth IRA, HSA. Read:
   IRS 590-A; Bogleheads "Traditional versus Roth".
4. What a stock, a bond, and a fund are. Read: FINRA investment products
   (https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investment-products);
   Investor.gov "Introduction to investing".
5. Index funds, expense ratios, and why costs compound. Read: Bogleheads
   "Three-fund portfolio" (https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Three-fund_portfolio);
   SEC investor bulletin on mutual fund fees.
6. Insurance of accounts: FDIC vs SIPC, and what neither covers (market
   losses). Read: FDIC and SIPC pages above.
7. Recognizing fraud and unsuitable products. Read: SEC Investor Alerts;
   FINRA BrokerCheck to verify anyone offering advice.

## Level 2: Building a portfolio

Goal: the learner can set an asset allocation, explain diversification
with numbers, rebalance, and place funds tax-efficiently.

1. Risk and return, standard deviation, drawdown. Read: MIT 15.401
   lectures on risk and return; Investor.gov "Assessing your risk tolerance".
2. Diversification and correlation; why 20 to 30 stocks are not a diversified
   portfolio in the market sense. Read: Markowitz (1952); Bogleheads
   "Asset allocation".
3. Asset allocation and glide paths; the role of bonds. Read: Bogleheads
   "Asset allocation"; TreasuryDirect for how Treasuries work.
4. Rebalancing: calendar vs threshold, and the cost of not doing it. Read:
   Bogleheads "Rebalancing"; Vanguard research on rebalancing (secondary).
5. Tax-efficient placement and tax-loss harvesting; the wash-sale rule.
   Read: Bogleheads "Tax-efficient fund placement"
   (https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Tax-efficient_fund_placement); IRS 550.
6. Dollar-cost averaging vs lump sum: what the evidence says (lump sum wins
   about two-thirds of the time; DCA is a behavioral tool). Read: Vanguard
   research (secondary); Bogleheads "Dollar cost averaging".
7. Behavioral finance: disposition effect, overtrading, recency, attention.
   Read: Shiller ECON 252 behavioral lectures; Odean (1998); Barber and
   Odean (2000).

## Level 3: Analysis and research

Goal: the learner can read a 10-K, compute basic ratios, explain a DCF and
a multiple, and judge an analyst claim.

1. Reading financial statements: income statement, balance sheet, cash flow;
   free cash flow. Read: SEC "Beginners' guide to financial statements"
   (https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguide);
   Damodaran's "Accounting 101" notes.
2. Ratios and what they miss: P/E, EV/EBITDA, ROIC, payout ratio, FFO for
   REITs. Read: Damodaran data pages; CFA Research Foundation briefs.
3. Discounted cash flow: assumptions, terminal value sensitivity, WACC.
   Read: Damodaran valuation class; the valuation skill's dcf-methods
   reference for the mechanics used here.
4. Dividends: yield vs total return, payout safety, dividend growth. Read:
   FINRA on dividends; the dividend-income skill's method notes.
5. Factor investing: size, value, momentum, quality; what premiums are
   documented and how they behave. Read: Fama and French (1992, 1993);
   Jegadeesh and Titman (1993); Carhart (1997).
6. Active vs passive: the arithmetic of active management; SPIVA
   scorecards. Read: Sharpe (1991); S&P SPIVA (secondary, methodology is
   public).
7. Evaluating a claim: benchmark, period, survivorship, fees, risk-adjusted
   return, and whether the claim is falsifiable.

## Level 4: Advanced topics

1. Options: pricing intuition, greeks, expected move, strategies and their
   risks. Read: Options Industry Council courses; Cboe education; Khan
   Academy options videos; the options skill's background notes.
2. Fixed income: duration, convexity, yield curve, credit spreads, TIPS and
   I bonds. Read: FINRA bond basics
   (https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investment-products/bonds);
   TreasuryDirect; Federal Reserve Education on interest rates.
3. Retirement decumulation: safe withdrawal rates and their assumptions.
   Read: Bengen (1994); Cooley, Hubbard and Walz (1998, "Trinity study");
   Bogleheads "Safe withdrawal rates".
4. Portfolio theory and CAPM, and their limits. Read: MIT 15.401 portfolio
   lectures; Fama and French (2004) "The CAPM: theory and evidence".
5. Real estate: mortgages, rent vs buy, cap rates, REIT metrics. Read: CFPB
   "Owning a home" (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/); Nareit
   FFO definition (https://www.reit.com/); the real-estate skill's notes.
6. Macro literacy: inflation, rates, the Fed's tools, reading FRED series.
   Read: Federal Reserve Education; Page One Economics; FRED.
7. Reading academic finance: how to find a paper on SSRN, what a t-statistic
   of 2 means, why out-of-sample results matter. Read: any Level 3 paper
   with its data appendix.

## Quiz bank

Ask one question at a time and read the options aloud; accept the letter or
the idea in the learner's own words. Each question has an id (for
`progress.py add --missed ID` and `list`'s `missed_history`), four options
with the correct one marked, a source, and a "Teach if missed" line naming
the section to re-teach and the core correction -- distractors encode a
named misconception from "Common misconceptions" where one applies.

Level 1

l1-q1. Q: You invest 10,000 at 7% a year. Roughly how much after 30 years?
- A) About 30,000 (treats the growth as simple, non-compounding interest)
- B) About 76,000 (1.07^30 = 7.61)
- C) About 140,000
- D) About 57,000 (the 6%-fee-adjusted figure from the worked example, not the 7% one)
Correct: B. Source: Investor.gov calculator.
Teach if missed: Level 1 item 1, compound interest -- growth compounds on
the prior year's total, not just the principal; a one-point fee difference
cut the 30-year outcome by about 25% in the worked example (76,123 vs
57,435 at 6%).

l1-q2. Q: What does an expense ratio of 1% cost on 100,000 per year?
- A) 100 (reads 1% of the wrong base)
- B) 1,000, charged once and never again
- C) 1,000 a year, recurring and compounding against the balance
- D) 10,000 (misplaced decimal)
Correct: C. Source: SEC fee bulletin.
Teach if missed: Level 1 item 5, index funds and expense ratios -- a fee is
a percentage taken every year, so it compounds against the balance the same
way returns do, only in the other direction.

l1-q3. Q: Does FDIC insurance cover losses in a brokerage account when
stocks fall?
- A) Yes -- FDIC insures brokerage accounts against market losses
- B) No -- FDIC covers bank deposits and SIPC covers missing assets when a broker fails; neither covers market losses
- C) No -- but SIPC covers market losses up to 500,000 (as of 2026)
- D) Yes, up to 250,000 per depositor, per bank (as of 2026)
Correct: B. Source: FDIC, SIPC.
Teach if missed: Level 1 item 6, insurance of accounts -- FDIC (bank
deposits) and SIPC (missing brokerage assets on broker failure) never cover
a decline in the market value of what you hold.

l1-q4. Q: Roth or traditional: which one taxes the money going in?
- A) Traditional
- B) Roth
- C) Both, equally, at contribution
- D) Neither; only capital gains are ever taxed
Correct: B. Source: IRS 590-A.
Teach if missed: Level 1 item 3, account types -- Roth contributions are
after-tax with tax-free qualified withdrawals; traditional contributions
are pre-tax with withdrawals taxed as ordinary income.

l1-q5. Q: What is an index fund?
- A) A fund a manager actively picks stocks for, aiming to beat an index
- B) A fund that holds a published index's securities in the index's weights, delivering the index return minus costs
- C) A fund guaranteed to return the same rate every year
- D) A fund that only holds bonds, to reduce volatility
Correct: B. Source: Investor.gov, Bogleheads.
Teach if missed: Level 1 item 5, index funds -- an index fund replicates a
published index's holdings and weights rather than picking winners, so its
return is the index return minus its costs.

Level 2

l2-q1. Q: Two assets each have 20% volatility and a correlation of 0. What
is the volatility of a 50/50 mix?
- A) 20% (assumes combining does not reduce risk)
- B) 14.1% (20% / sqrt 2)
- C) 10% (assumes risk is cut in half, as if fully hedged)
- D) 28.3% (multiplies by sqrt 2 instead of dividing)
Correct: B. Source: Markowitz; MIT 15.401.
Teach if missed: Level 2 item 2, diversification and correlation -- combining
uncorrelated assets reduces volatility by a square-root relationship, not a
simple average; this is the mechanism behind the misconception that owning
several holdings alone is diversification.

l2-q2. Q: What is the wash-sale rule?
- A) A loss is disallowed if you buy a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale; the loss is added to the new lot's basis
- B) A loss can never be claimed if you ever re-buy the same security
- C) It only applies to the exact same ticker within the same calendar year
- D) It doubles the capital-gains tax rate on the repurchased shares
Correct: A. Source: IRS 550.
Teach if missed: Level 2 item 5, tax-loss harvesting -- the wash-sale window
is 30 days before or after the sale on a substantially identical security,
and the disallowed loss becomes part of the replacement lot's cost basis,
not a permanent forfeiture or a rate penalty.

l2-q3. Q: Your target is 60/40 and stocks have run to 70/30. What does
rebalancing do to expected return and risk?
- A) Sells bonds to buy more stocks, chasing the recent trend
- B) Sells stocks to buy bonds, lowering expected return and risk back to the target; a risk-control decision, not a return forecast
- C) Nothing, until the drift exceeds 50 percentage points
- D) Increases expected return by locking in the stock gains
Correct: B. Source: Bogleheads "Rebalancing".
Teach if missed: Level 2 item 4, rebalancing -- rebalancing sells the
outperforming asset to restore the target mix; it manages risk to a chosen
level and does not forecast returns.

l2-q4. Q: Which is more tax-efficient in a taxable account: a bond fund or
a broad stock index fund?
- A) The bond fund, because bonds are safe
- B) The stock index fund -- low turnover, qualified dividends and deferred gains; bonds go in tax-advantaged accounts when possible
- C) They are equally tax-efficient
- D) The bond fund, because interest income is taxed at the long-term capital-gains rate
Correct: B. Source: Bogleheads tax-efficient placement.
Teach if missed: Level 2 item 5, tax-efficient fund placement -- a broad
stock index fund's low turnover and qualified dividends make it more
tax-efficient in a taxable account than a bond fund, whose interest is
generally taxed as ordinary income; the "bonds are safe" idea does not make
them tax-efficient.

l2-q5. Q: What is the disposition effect?
- A) Holding both winners and losers equally long, out of patience
- B) Selling losers quickly and letting winners run
- C) Selling winners too early and holding losers too long; Odean measured investors realizing gains at about 1.5 times the rate they realized losses
- D) A rule requiring brokers to sell a losing position after 30 days
Correct: C. Source: Odean (1998).
Teach if missed: Level 2 item 7, behavioral finance -- the disposition
effect is selling winners too early and holding losers too long, documented
by Odean (1998) at roughly a 1.5-to-1 realization ratio.

Level 3

l3-q1. Q: Free cash flow, in one line?
- A) Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures: cash the business generates after maintaining and growing its assets
- B) Net income plus depreciation
- C) Revenue minus all operating expenses, before taxes
- D) Operating cash flow plus capital expenditures
Correct: A. Source: SEC beginners' guide; Damodaran.
Teach if missed: Level 3 item 1, reading financial statements -- free cash
flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures, not net income plus
depreciation or a sign-flipped version of the same subtraction.

l3-q2. Q: A company reports operating cash flow of 500M and capital
expenditures of 150M. What is its free cash flow?
- A) 500M (ignores capex)
- B) 350M
- C) 650M (adds instead of subtracts)
- D) 150M (reports capex as if it were FCF)
Correct: B. Source: SEC beginners' guide; Damodaran.
Teach if missed: Level 3 item 1, reading financial statements -- free cash
flow subtracts capital expenditures from operating cash flow (500M minus
150M is 350M); adding them or dropping capex are the two most common slips.

l3-q3. Q: Why does a small change in the discount rate move a DCF value so
much?
- A) Most of the value sits in the terminal value, which divides by (WACC minus growth); a small change in a small denominator is a large change
- B) The discount rate only affects the first year's cash flow
- C) DCF values are insensitive to the discount rate past year five
- D) A higher discount rate always increases the valuation, rewarding patience
Correct: A. Source: Damodaran; valuation skill reference.
Teach if missed: Level 3 item 3, discounted cash flow -- the terminal
value divides by (WACC minus growth), so a small change in a small
denominator moves the whole valuation a lot.

l3-q4. Q: What does Sharpe's arithmetic of active management say?
- A) Active managers as a group beat the market before fees, because of better information
- B) Before costs, the average active dollar earns the market; after costs it earns less, so the average active investor must underperform the index
- C) Active management always underperforms, even before any fees are charged
- D) Passive funds outperform because they hold cash reserves active funds do not
Correct: B. Source: Sharpe (1991).
Teach if missed: Level 3 item 6, active vs passive -- Sharpe (1991)'s point
is an accounting identity: gross of costs the active and passive dollar
earn the same market return, so costs alone make the average active dollar
underperform net of fees.

l3-q5. Q: A fund shows 15% a year for 5 years. What do you ask before being
impressed?
- A) Nothing further -- five years at 15% is definitionally a skilled manager
- B) Versus which benchmark, over which period, with what risk, after which fees, and whether other funds from the same family were closed (survivorship)
- C) Only whether the fund beat inflation over the period
- D) Whether the fund's ticker is easy to remember
Correct: B. Source: Level 3 item 7.
Teach if missed: Level 3 item 7, evaluating a claim -- a return number alone
says nothing without its benchmark, period, risk, fees and survivorship;
past fund performance predicting future performance is weak and mostly
explained by costs and momentum (Carhart 1997).

Level 4

l4-q1. Q: What is the expected move implied by a 30% IV over 30 days on a
100 stock?
- A) About 8.6 (100 x 0.30 x sqrt(30/365))
- B) About 30.0 (uses the annual IV directly as the 30-day move)
- C) About 2.5 (divides IV by 12 like a monthly rate, skipping the square-root-of-time scaling)
- D) About 100.0 (expects the stock to double or go to zero)
Correct: A. Source: options skill.
Teach if missed: Level 4 item 1, options -- expected move scales with the
square root of time, not linearly, so a 30% annualized IV implies roughly
an 8.6% move over 30 days, not 30% or a naive monthly fraction of it.

l4-q2. Q: What does duration of 7 tell you?
- A) The bond's price does not respond to rates for its first 7 years
- B) A 1-point rise in yields moves the bond's price down about 7%
- C) The bond pays a 7% coupon
- D) The bond matures in exactly 7 years
Correct: B. Source: FINRA bond basics.
Teach if missed: Level 4 item 2, fixed income -- duration approximates the
percent price change for a 1-point yield move; it is not the coupon rate or
the maturity date.

l4-q3. Q: Which of these carries no credit risk if held to maturity?
- A) A corporate bond
- B) A US Treasury bond
- C) A high-yield bond
- D) A municipal bond
Correct: B. Source: TreasuryDirect; FINRA bond basics.
Teach if missed: Level 4 item 2, fixed income -- all bonds carry rate and
inflation risk, and every issuer but the US Treasury carries credit
(default) risk; only a Treasury held to maturity avoids credit risk on
principal, correcting the "bonds are safe" misconception.

l4-q4. Q: A trader buys a call expecting the stock to rise 5% by
expiration, and it does -- but implied volatility collapses right after the
move. What is the most likely result?
- A) The option's value rises in lockstep with the stock's percent move, since options are just leverage
- B) The option could still lose value despite the correct direction, from volatility crush and time decay
- C) The option's value is unaffected by volatility and depends only on the stock price
- D) The option automatically gains whenever the call is in the money, regardless of other factors
Correct: B. Source: Options Industry Council; Cboe education.
Teach if missed: Level 4 item 1, options -- options carry time decay and
volatility exposure alongside directional exposure, so a right-direction
trade can still lose money; this corrects the "options are just leverage"
misconception.

l4-q5. Q: What did Bengen's 4% rule assume?
- A) Any stock/bond mix works as long as withdrawals never adjust for inflation
- B) A 50-75% stock portfolio, 30-year horizon, inflation-adjusted withdrawals, US historical data from 1926; a historical worst-case, not a guarantee
- C) A guarantee that a portfolio never runs out of money at any withdrawal rate up to 4%
- D) A rule set by the IRS for minimum retirement account withdrawals
Correct: B. Source: Bengen (1994); Trinity study.
Teach if missed: Level 4 item 3, retirement decumulation -- Bengen's result
is a historical stress test under specific assumptions, not a guarantee or
an IRS-mandated withdrawal rule; the required-minimum-distribution rules
are a separate, unrelated requirement (see the retirement skill).

## Common misconceptions

- "Diversified" means owning several stocks: no; diversification is about
  exposure across the whole market and asset classes, and correlation.
- A high dividend yield means a safe or cheap stock: yield rises when the
  price falls; check payout and cash flow first.
- Past fund performance predicts future performance: persistence is weak
  and mostly explained by costs and momentum (Carhart 1997).
- Dollar-cost averaging beats lump-sum investing: on average it does not;
  it reduces regret, not expected return.
- Bonds are safe: they carry rate, credit, and inflation risk; only
  Treasuries held to maturity avoid credit and rate risk on principal.
- Options are just leverage: they also carry time decay and volatility
  exposure; a correct direction can still lose money.
- The 4% rule is a plan: it is a historical stress test; sequence risk and
  fees change the answer.
- Market timing based on news works: individual investors who trade on
  attention-grabbing news underperform (Barber and Odean 2008).

## Session formats

- Placement (5 minutes): three quiz questions, one per level starting at
  the learner's stated level; place them and propose the next three items.
- Spaced review (start of a returning session): re-quiz concepts from a
  session two or more sessions ago before teaching anything new -- prefer
  quiz ids already in the missed history, then any other concept from that
  older session; each question's "Teach if missed" line is the correction
  to give on a repeat miss.
- Concept lesson (10 minutes): definition, worked number, source, check
  question, recap.
- Portfolio walk-through (with consent): run portfolio-snapshot or
  dividend-income, then explain each figure on the page as a concept,
  without commenting on whether the holdings are good.
- Paper reading (20 minutes): abstract, data, main table, what would
  falsify it, what it implies and does not imply for an individual.
