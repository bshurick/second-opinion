# Account Rules — 401(k), IRA, HSA, FSA, 529

> **Figures for tax year 2026; verify current-year limits.** Every dollar
> limit below is marked "(2026)" and most of them are inflation-indexed and
> change annually (the IRS typically announces the following year's
> retirement-plan limits in October/November, and HSA limits in the spring).
> Before quoting any figure here to a user in a year after 2026, run a
> WebSearch for "IRS [account type] contribution limits [current year]" and
> use the current figure instead — never state a limit from this file as
> current without checking. The mechanics (deduction rules, tax treatment,
> penalties) change far less often than the dollar amounts, but SECURE 2.0
> and 2025 legislation (P.L. 119-21) provisions are still phasing in and
> worth a periodic check too. Sources: IRS IR-2025-111 (Nov. 13, 2025) and
> Notice 2025-67 for retirement plans and IRAs; Rev. Proc. 2025-19 for HSAs;
> Rev. Proc. 2025-32 for the health FSA and the gift exclusion; IRS
> Publication 15-B (2026) for dependent care.

## 401(k) / 403(b) / 457(b)

- Employee elective-deferral limit: **$24,500 (2026)** combined
  across traditional (pre-tax) and Roth 401(k)/403(b) contributions to one
  employer's plan(s).
- Catch-up contribution, age 50+: an additional **$8,000 (2026)**,
  for a total of **$32,500 (2026)**.
- SECURE 2.0 "super catch-up," ages 60-63 only: an additional
  **$11,250 (2026)** instead of the standard catch-up, for a total of
  **$35,750 (2026)**. Reverts to the standard catch-up the year the
  participant turns 64.
- Roth-only catch-up for higher earners (SECURE 2.0, from 2026): a
  participant whose prior-year FICA wages (Form W-2 box 3) from the plan's
  employer exceeded **$150,000 (2026, based on 2025 wages)** must make any
  catch-up contributions as Roth; a plan without a Roth option cannot take
  their catch-up at all. The threshold is indexed (**$155,000** applies to
  2026 wages for 2027).
- Combined employee + employer contribution limit (415(c) limit), all
  sources including match and profit-sharing: **$72,000 (2026)**, or
  **$80,000 (2026)** with the standard catch-up, or **$83,250 (2026)** with
  the age-60-63 super catch-up (catch-ups sit on top of the 415(c) limit).
- Tax mechanics: traditional contributions are pre-tax (reduce current
  taxable income); growth is tax-deferred; withdrawals in retirement are
  taxed as ordinary income. Roth 401(k) contributions are after-tax;
  qualified withdrawals (age 59½+ and the account open 5+ years) are
  entirely tax-free, including growth. Employer match, when offered, is
  pre-tax by default regardless of whether the employee's own
  contributions are traditional or Roth (SECURE 2.0 lets a plan offer
  Roth employer contributions, taxed in the year made, if it opts in), and it is not subject to the
  employee elective-deferral limit (only to the combined 415(c) limit
  above).
- Early-withdrawal penalty: 10% additional tax on the taxable portion of a
  withdrawal before age 59½, on top of ordinary income tax, with statutory
  exceptions (e.g., separation from service at age 55+ for that employer's
  plan, disability, certain medical expenses, qualified birth/adoption up
  to $5,000, and others). Required minimum distributions (RMDs) currently
  begin at age 73.
- 457(b) plans have their own separate elective-deferral limit (mirrors the
  401(k)/403(b) figure above) and, notably, no 10% early-withdrawal penalty
  on distributions after separation from service, though ordinary income
  tax still applies.

## Traditional and Roth IRA

- Combined contribution limit across all of a person's traditional and
  Roth IRAs: **$7,500 (2026)**.
- Catch-up contribution, age 50+: an additional **$1,100 (2026)**,
  for a total of **$8,600 (2026)**. SECURE 2.0 indexed this catch-up to
  inflation starting in 2024 (in $100 steps), so it now moves too.
- Contributions cannot exceed the account owner's taxable compensation for
  the year; a non-working spouse can contribute based on the working
  spouse's compensation (a "spousal IRA").
- Traditional IRA deductibility: fully deductible if neither spouse is
  covered by a workplace retirement plan. If the contributor is covered,
  the deduction phases out over MAGI **$81,000-$91,000 (2026)**
  single or head of household, **$129,000-$149,000 (2026)** married filing
  jointly, or **$0-$10,000** married filing separately. If the contributor
  is not covered but their spouse is, the phase-out is
  **$242,000-$252,000 (2026)** married filing jointly. Non-deductible contributions are
  still allowed above these ranges and create basis (tracked on IRS Form
  8606) that is not re-taxed on withdrawal.
- Roth IRA eligibility phases out by MAGI regardless of workplace-plan
  coverage: **$153,000-$168,000 (2026)** single or head of household,
  **$242,000-$252,000 (2026)** married filing jointly, or **$0-$10,000**
  married filing separately. Above the top
  of the range, direct Roth contributions are not allowed (a "backdoor
  Roth" — non-deductible traditional contribution converted to Roth — is a
  common workaround, but has its own pro-rata tax complications when other
  pre-tax traditional IRA balances exist).
- Tax mechanics: traditional IRA follows the same pre-tax-in,
  taxed-on-withdrawal pattern as a traditional 401(k). Roth IRA follows the
  same after-tax-in, tax-free-qualified-withdrawal pattern as a Roth
  401(k), with one added flexibility: contributions (not earnings) can be
  withdrawn at any time, tax- and penalty-free, since they were already
  taxed going in.
- Early-withdrawal penalty: 10% additional tax on the taxable portion
  before age 59½, with exceptions including a first-time home purchase (up
  to $10,000 lifetime), qualified education expenses, and substantially
  equal periodic payments (72(t)). Traditional IRA RMDs begin at age 73;
  Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the original owner's lifetime.

## HSA (Health Savings Account)

- Requires enrollment in a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP).
  2026 HDHP minimum annual deductible: **$1,700 (2026)** self-only /
  **$3,400 (2026)** family. 2026 HDHP maximum out-of-pocket:
  **$8,500 (2026)** self-only / **$17,000 (2026)** family.
- HSA contribution limit: **$4,400 (2026)** self-only coverage /
  **$8,750 (2026)** family coverage (employer contributions count
  toward this limit).
- Catch-up contribution, age 55+: an additional **$1,000 (2026)**,
  fixed by statute (not inflation-indexed) and contributed to an account in
  the older spouse's own name if both spouses want to catch up.
- Tax mechanics: the only "triple tax advantage" account in this list —
  contributions are pre-tax (or deductible if made outside payroll),
  growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are
  tax-free at any age, with no use-it-or-lose-it deadline and full
  portability between employers. After age 65, non-medical withdrawals are
  taxed as ordinary income (like a traditional IRA) but avoid the 20%
  penalty that applies below 65.
- Early non-medical withdrawal penalty: 20% additional tax plus ordinary
  income tax before age 65.

## FSA (Flexible Spending Account)

- Health care FSA employee contribution limit: **$3,400 (2026)**.
  This limit is inflation-indexed.
- Health care FSA carryover: employers may allow up to
  **$680 (2026)** of unused funds to roll into the next plan year
  (an employer choice, not a guarantee — some plans instead offer a
  short grace period, and some offer neither, forfeiting unused funds).
- Dependent care FSA limit: **$7,500 (2026)** per household
  (**$3,750 (2026)** if married filing separately), raised from $5,000 /
  $2,500 by P.L. 119-21 starting in 2026. Unlike the health care FSA, this
  limit is fixed by statute and is not inflation-indexed, so it changes
  only when Congress amends it. The exclusion also cannot exceed the
  smaller of the two spouses' earned incomes.
- Tax mechanics: contributions are made pre-tax through payroll, reducing
  taxable income; there is no tax-deferred growth (FSA balances do not
  invest) and no early-withdrawal penalty concept, since funds are only
  ever used for qualifying expenses. A health care FSA is generally
  incompatible with contributing to an HSA in the same period unless it is
  a limited-purpose FSA (dental/vision only) or a post-deductible FSA.

## 529 education savings plans

- No federal annual contribution limit. Contributions are treated as
  gifts to the beneficiary, so they are capped in practice by the annual
  gift-tax exclusion: **$19,000 (2026)** per donor per beneficiary
  before using any lifetime gift/estate-tax exemption, or **$38,000
  (2026)** for a married couple splitting gifts.
- 5-year "superfunding" election: a donor may contribute up to 5 years'
  worth of the annual exclusion at once — **$95,000 (2026)** per
  donor (**$190,000 (2026)** for a married couple) — treated as
  spread evenly over 5 years for gift-tax purposes, with no further gifts
  to that beneficiary from that donor during the period.
- Aggregate account balance caps are set by the state that sponsors the
  plan, not by federal law, and vary widely — commonly in the
  **roughly $300,000-$550,000+ (2026)** range depending on the state
  — so always confirm the specific plan's cap rather than quoting a single
  figure.
- Tax mechanics: contributions are after-tax at the federal level (some
  states offer a state income-tax deduction or credit for contributions to
  that state's own plan); growth and withdrawals are federally tax-free
  when used for qualified education expenses (tuition, fees, room and
  board, books, K-12 tuition and related K-12 costs up to $20,000/year from 2026, up from $10,000, and registered
  apprenticeship costs). Non-qualified withdrawals owe ordinary income tax
  plus a 10% penalty on the earnings portion only (contributions come out
  tax- and penalty-free, since they were after-tax going in).
- SECURE 2.0 Roth IRA rollover: unused 529 funds can roll to the
  beneficiary's own Roth IRA, up to a **$35,000** lifetime
  cap, subject to the beneficiary's annual Roth IRA contribution limit for
  the year (see above), the account must have been open 15+ years, and
  contributions/earnings from the last 5 years are not eligible to roll.
